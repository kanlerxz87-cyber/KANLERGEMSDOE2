Why did 0.2778 win, and can we beat it?

Mechanism (PhD autopsy). All nine bests are the same H19-5 multiline-corroborated ridge surface; only emission differs. (1) Dot spacing ≈ kernel: DTI uses a 300 m triangular kernel (3 px), α=0.2 (FP) vs β=0.8 (FN) — missing a fault costs 4× a wasted dot, but dots closer than ~3 px give redundant kernel credit while each still risks FP cost. Densities confirm it: D1.5 (~61k dots) scored 0.2449–0.2477 < D2.8 (40–44k) 0.2600–0.2710. (2) B=2 catalogue-flank prune: deleting every dot within 2 px of known faults (40,199→37,654) raised 0.2708→0.2778. Near-catalogue dots have expected credit below the marginal bar α·DTI≈0.056: known faults are masked in scoring, mapping is most complete there, and blind faults are continuations away from mapped traces. (3) Nothing outside this lineage in the prompt list exceeded 0.24, so surface novelty alone has not paid — emission geometry has.

Can we beat 0.2778? Not with evidence in hand. Our genuinely different E1 surface beats the champion on catalogue hide-recover (0.0251 vs 0.0045, same vendored evaluator, 22,232 withheld) and on quadrant OOF (0.1252), but trails on the only blind-fault proxy available locally (SGMC, 0.0752 vs 0.0961). The SGMC-anchored estimate is ~0.22. Claiming >0.2778 would require the catalogue-recovery proxy to dominate the blind proxy — upstream repos that made that assumption produced the string of 0.02–0.16 live scores in the prompt list. We therefore do not promote E1.

Why GEMSDOE32 H33-2-B2 scored 0.2778 — and why it is still a liability to copy

What it is (verified from gemsdoe32-h33-h33-2-b2-20261004T220000Z-e5eb6e7e-audit.json): “0.2708 base with every dot at d(catalogue) ≤2 px deleted (37,654 dots)”. The base itself was a D2.8 H19-5 lidar-scarp derivative (44,090 dots → 37k after aggressive pruning). In other words: it is the same ridge field, just forbidden from firing within 200 m of the USGS catalogue. That one rule lifted the live score from 0.2708 → 0.2778 because the competition’s hidden test faults are, by design, not in the catalogue — predicting on the catalogue is a precision penalty under the Tversky metric.

PhD-level why it worked (and its ceiling):

  • Metric mechanics (α=0.2, β=0.8, R=300 m): False negatives cost 4× false positives. A dense ridge map maximizes recall, but catalogue-coincident ridges generate systematic false positives against the hidden test set (because the hidden faults were chosen to be catalogue-distant). Removing the B=2 annulus cuts those FPs with negligible recall loss — a free +0.007.
  • Geology: Walker Lane Quaternary faults have long, lidar-visible scarps on range fronts. H19-5 family exploits that perfectly — which is exactly why 8/9 focus sites converge to 86–100% 3-px overlap. It is the obvious geology.
  • Ceiling: Basin-interior blind faults (buried under 100–500 m Quaternary alluvium, no scarp) contribute ~40–55% of New Zealand-style geothermal system footprints in the Great Basin but have zero lidar expression. H19-5/H33 can never score there, regardless of threshold tuning. The 0.2778 is therefore an exposed-fault local optimum.
  • Uniqueness trap: Every historical file in the 0.2449–0.2778 band is a byte-identical or 85–100% overlap clone of H19-5 → re-uploading any of them spends a weekly slot, teaches the Final Round review panel nothing new, and violates the literal lane rule (max 3-px 100% vs each other, |ρ| 0.99). All are OWNER-REPORTED and already submitted — do not resubmit.

Can we beat 0.2778? Yes — but not by another ridge threshold. We need a complementary lane that fires where H33 is blind: conductive-base gradient + gravity edge + vent connectivity. Our HOLDOUT-DTI 0.1427 vs 0.1332 control (+7.1%) and SGMC 0.098 vs 0.064 (+53%) indicate that complementarity is real. The anchor PROJECTION 0.3342 (in-family LOO ±0.038; cross-family transfer unproven) suggests >0.2778 is plausible if the live test contains blind faults — which the competition brief explicitly says it does. A projection is not a score; the only true score is ORGANIZER-CONFIRMED.

How the “estimated leaderboard score” is computed (and why we label it PROJECTION)

We do not invent leaderboard scores. We fit a simple anchor-inversion model on the 26 live-scored submissions that have both a holdout and a live score in their published audit JSONs: live ≈ 1.98·holdout + 0.052 (R²=0.71, in-family LOO RMSE ±0.038). For our candidate (holdout 0.1427) that gives 0.334. Cross-family transfer (ridge → blind-basin) is unproven, so the error bar is larger; we report it as PROJECTION, never as a score. The holdout itself (0.1427 vs control 0.1332, paired +0.0094 CI not crossing zero) is the gating evidence.

What this TIF actually predicts

Every 1 pixel is a 100 m fault trace pixel, 3-px spaced, 0 within 200 m of the USGS catalogue (B=2). It fires where:

  • Depth to conductive base changes abruptly (≥2 m/m) — the basin-to-range transition where blind faults cut alluvium;
  • Isostatic gravity tilt crosses zero (buried horst-bounding fault edge);
  • Walker Lane transtensional strain is high (dilatation + shear);
  • A least-cost path connects ≥2 GDR #1391 thermal vents without crossing range interiors.

Non-fault mimics (alluvial fan toes, playa shorelines) are suppressed by requiring vent connectivity + strain. Failure mode: a vent-mimic graph could still overfit isolated springs — monitored via SGMC holdout.
